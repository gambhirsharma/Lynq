01Executive Summary

Lynq NTN-Pulse is a software-defined digital twin and policy engine for OQ Technology's regenerative gNB-onboard LEO constellation. It governs industrial traffic across onboard gNB, feeder/ISL paths, gateways, 5G Core and cloud, applying 3GPP-aligned policies for QoS, latency, availability, data residency, spectrum and cost. The first beachhead is offshore energy and maritime operations: Lynq adds the software layer that makes regenerative NTN capacity measurable and deployable.

02Problem & Opportunity

Status quo: the bent-pipe limit. Transparent payloads need a visible ground gateway and feeder link for processing, routing and core access. If the gateway is out of view, blocked, nationally restricted or overloaded, service degrades or is lost. Offshore wind farms, platforms, vessels and pipelines are hit hardest, and their traffic is mixed: alarms and control commands need priority; telemetry, video and files tolerate delay.

A regenerative gNB moves RAN and Layer 2/3 functions into orbit but adds Doppler/delay, handovers, intermittent feeder access, gateway selection, ISL routing and compute limits. Lynq's policy layer decides whether traffic is delivered now, routed, edge-processed or stored for later downlink.

03Proposed Direction

Technical angle

  • Digital twin: one model for today's ~12-satellite fleet and the planned 30: satellite position, coverage, gateway visibility, feeder/ISL state, terminal location, traffic demand and onboard compute.
  • Policy engine: assigns traffic classes to available paths by QoS, latency, reliability, residency and cost.
  • NTN model and dashboard: timing, beam management, handover, link budgets, scheduling, queues, predicted outages and route decisions.

Constellation (sized with the twin). We start with today's ~12 satellites (OQ's 10 + 2 ERMIS) and size the twin for the planned 30, focused on one region: the North Sea offshore energy basin (UK, Norwegian, Danish, Dutch and German sectors, ~51–61°N). At ~500–550 km and 10° minimum elevation, one footprint is ~3,000 km across, so one satellite in view covers the whole basin and every land gateway around it. Twelve satellites give frequent passes, not continuous coverage: alarms go out on the next pass, bulk data by store-and-forward. At a 5-year life, a 30-satellite fleet needs ~6 replacements per year. Scaling to OQ's planned 30 (3 planes × 10) shrinks gaps about 2.5× and lets each plane close an RF inter-satellite ring; in our twin, a 65° Walker delta instead of sun-synchronous planes cuts the worst North Sea gap from 118 to 3 min. The same policies then add mesh relaying and extend to the Norwegian Sea and Atlantic, on the way to OQ's 100 D2D payloads.

Payload trade. Full regenerative gNB: bent-pipe cannot serve users with no gateway in view, and split gNB (options 2/7.2x) keeps most RF/PHY power onboard while RRC still round-trips over the feeder. Power, thermal and radiation costs are mitigated by a narrowband gNB, duty-cycling over empty ocean and COTS processing under a rad-hard supervisor. The scheduler must sit in orbit because the gNB sorts every message into three classes: critical (alarms, safety/shutdown commands, a few hundred bytes) takes the first uplink slot and first authorised path on the current pass; regular (telemetry, logs, files) is queued onboard and store-and-forwarded on a later pass (≤90 min); metadata (device status, position, delivery receipts, policy tags) is tiny but tells the twin and policy engine where each asset is and which gateway it may use.

Example: mid-Atlantic platform → Frankfurt cloud in ~75–110 ms one-way over ISL, versus no service on bent-pipe.

ISL trade: optical offers high capacity and low interference but needs precise pointing and tracking; RF (Ka/V/W-band) adds spectrum and interference constraints. Narrowband traffic needs only single-digit Mbps per satellite, so we choose RF intra-plane ISL on all satellites (near-static geometry, proven by Iridium) and optical cross-plane on a relay subset (no spectrum licence, hard to intercept), from European suppliers to avoid ITAR/EAR.

Doppler/delay pre-compensation follows 3GPP Rel-17/18/19 procedures. AI forecasts traffic, detects anomalies and prioritises resources; deterministic timing, link-budget and safety controls stay authoritative.

Regulatory angle

The Consultant's Dilemma: a local ground gateway may be technically attractive but unavailable because of national restrictions, data-residency rules or licensing. A regenerative gNB knows the user's identity, PLMN and verified location, so Lynq applies routing policy onboard and sends each flow over an authorised ISL/feeder path only to a policy-compliant gateway with local user-plane breakout. Under MNO partnerships, the MNO keeps lawful intercept.

Compliance treats ITU RR Article 9 coordination and Article 22 EPFD limits as hard design constraints (feeder-link masks, gateway exclusion zones), with PFD/EIRP conditions and national authorisations. It supports EU-hosted processing, encryption, key ownership, pseudonymisation and GDPR retention. Sovereignty is built into routing.

Business angle

The first buyer is a European offshore energy operator or offshore wind O&M provider (e.g., Equinor, Ørsted or Eni), aligned with OQ's European funding focus. Lynq reduces blind spots, improves alarm delivery and cuts dependence on VSAT and fixed gateways.

Revenue: integration fees, monthly subscriptions by asset and traffic class, usage charges for data/priority QoS, and MNO wholesale or roaming. For the initial North Sea network, opex including replenishment is ~€9M/yr: break-even at ~75k offshore assets at €120/yr, or ~9k sovereign/defence endpoints at €1,000+/yr. At ~€2–3/MB we price per asset, never per byte. OQ's EIC Accelerator award (€2.5M grant plus up to €15M equity) and €25M EIB venture debt tied to 20+ launches within two years fund a second service on the same network: SOS and safety messaging to unmodified phones for offshore crews and vessels, alongside asset IoT. TCO is benchmarked against VSAT, private LTE, Sateliot, Skylo, Starlink D2C/Lynk and Iridium.
